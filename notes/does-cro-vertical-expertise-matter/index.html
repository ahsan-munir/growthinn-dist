Does your CRO specialist need to know your category?

The same 56 job posts, read again for whether category knowledge is asked for.

On this page

  1. What buyers ask for
  2. Does the method travel?
  3. A test for any specialist’s pitch
  4. What 56 job posts can’t tell you

The short version

Ten of 56 Shopify job posts I read asked for experience in the buyer’s own category. Only one made it a hard requirement. The useful split is not how many asked — it is why: four named something the category would actually change (a compliance constraint, how customers decide, who the audience is, what closes the sale), and six only asked for a portfolio that looks like theirs. That difference is the test. When someone says they specialise in your category, ask what they would do differently on your store because of it — and listen for which of the four kinds of answer you get.

Someone typed this into Google, word for word:

“do cro agencies that specialize in fitness and wellness dtc actually approach things differently than general ecommerce cro shops? trying to figure out if vertical expertise matters or if good testing methodology travels across categories”

It is a good question, and the search results do not really answer it. When I checked in September 2026, the first page was agency lists and agencies’ own pages — “best CRO agencies for wellness brands” and similar. Each one has a category to sell.

Read this first

I work as a CRO consultant, so I am not neutral about how specialists are chosen. Rather than give you my opinion, the evidence below is what buyers themselves wrote when they were hiring — quoted rather than summarised, so you can weigh it without taking my word for it.

What buyers ask for

In September 2026 I read 56 Shopify-related job posts from my Upwork feed and copied each one’s wording before writing anything about it. Ten of them asked for experience in the buyer’s own category.

Only one made it a hard requirement:

“No apparel work = no reply.”

The other nine said preferred, ideally, nice to have, or asked for examples. So even the buyers who ask mostly treat category as a bonus rather than a filter.

The more useful split is why they asked. Four posts said what the category would actually change.

The four reasons that name something real

A constraint — things you are not allowed to say or do in that category:

“Experience in healthcare, telehealth, or another compliance-sensitive industry”

How the customer buys:

“You understand that people buying ornaments are buying a feeling, not a product spec”

The audience:

“products targeting adults 40+”

What decides the sale in that category:

“You know what makes a clothing store convert: sizing clarity, real photos, returns visible, fast on mobile”

The six that asked for a portfolio

The other six only asked for work that looks like theirs:

“Please send three links, ideally live sites, for fashion or premium lifestyle brands.”

“Which two live Shopify apparel stores best demonstrate your relevant experience?”

There is nothing wrong with that. But a matching portfolio tells you someone has worked on stores like yours. It does not tell you what they would do differently on yours.

Does the method travel?

One post had both halves in it, which is why I keep coming back to it.

It was an agency that builds stores for clothing brands. It required apparel experience — a hard filter. And in the same post, it described the work as a “CRO pass on every new client store off our checklist”: offer above the fold, social proof, a trust row for shipping and returns, a sticky add-to-cart on mobile.

The checklist is the same for every store. The category knowledge — sizing, real photos, visible returns — is listed separately, as something you are expected to bring on top.

So in that one post, the method travels and the category knowledge sits beside it. It does not replace it.

That is one post, not a rule. A single brief cannot settle whether methodology travels across categories — it can only show how one agency, hiring for its own clothing work, chose to split the two.

A test for any specialist’s pitch

When someone tells you they specialise in your category, ask one question:

“What would you do differently on my store because of it?”

Then listen for which kind of answer you get:

Four kinds of answer

  • A constraint — claims you cannot make, rules about what may be shown or promised, regulated wording. These are real, and a generalist can get them wrong without knowing.
  • A buying behaviour — how people in your category decide. Sizing and returns for clothing; a feeling rather than a spec for gifts. These change what goes on the page first.
  • An audience — who you are selling to, and what that means for how the page reads.
  • A portfolio — "we have done ten stores like yours." That is experience, but it is not an answer to the question. Ask again.

If the answer is in the first three, the category experience is doing work you can check. If it is only the fourth, you are paying for familiarity — which may still be worth something, but you should know that is what it is.

This is my reading of what the four reason-giving posts named, not a rule any of them stated.

What 56 job posts can’t tell you

Whether the specialists who were hired for their category did better than the ones who were not. Job posts show what buyers asked for, not how it turned out.

And ten posts from one freelancer’s feed is a small sample, not the market.
